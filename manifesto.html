MANIFESTO

Faith • Justice • Solidarity

We begin with a simple conviction:
The economy was made for humanity, not humanity for the economy.

Every human being possesses a dignity that cannot be measured by wealth, productivity, class or social position. Economic life must therefore be judged by how well it serves human beings and the communities in which they live.

The Gospel Has Social Consequences

Christian faith cannot be confined to private morality. Love of neighbour has consequences for the institutions that shape our common life.

Feeding the hungry is an act of love. So is asking why hunger persists amid abundance. Caring for those harmed by injustice is necessary. So is examining the structures that repeatedly produce injustice.

Charity responds to suffering. Justice also asks what produced it.

We Must Understand the World We Seek to Change

Moral conviction without serious analysis is insufficient. Questions of ownership, labour, class, bargaining power and the concentration of economic power must be studied honestly.

Gospel Socialism therefore learns from the tradition of socialist economic analysis, including Marx's critique of capitalism, without accepting philosophical materialism, historical determinism or the belief that history inevitably produces socialism.

No theory is beyond criticism. Economic ideas must remain accountable to evidence and human experience.

Economic Power Should Be Democratic

Political democracy is incomplete when enormous areas of economic life remain governed by concentrations of power over which ordinary people exercise little meaningful influence.

Workers should have a genuine voice in the institutions that shape their lives. Gospel Socialism therefore supports the exploration of institutions such as trade unions, cooperatives, employee ownership, workplace democracy, profit sharing and other forms of broadly distributed economic power.

The objective is not simply to transfer power from private corporations to the state. It is to distribute power more widely throughout society.

Neither the Market Nor the State Is Sacred

Markets can coordinate economic activity, encourage innovation and allow individuals and communities considerable freedom. They can also concentrate wealth and power or fail to provide essential goods fairly.

Government can protect the vulnerable, provide public goods and restrain concentrated private power. Government itself can also become bureaucratic, coercive or unaccountable.

Gospel Socialism therefore treats both markets and governments as tools rather than objects of devotion.

Property Carries Social Obligations

Personal property and individual initiative have legitimate places in human life. But ownership that grants substantial power over workers, communities or essential resources carries obligations to the wider society.

The greater the economic power attached to property, the greater the responsibility accompanying it.

Democracy and Liberty Are Essential

Gospel Socialism rejects authoritarian socialism. Multiparty democracy, civil liberties, freedom of conscience, independent institutions, a free press and the right to organize are not temporary conveniences on the road to a better society.

They are expressions of human dignity and safeguards against the corruption of power.

Justice Without Hatred

Economic conflict is real. Employers and workers, owners and tenants, creditors and debtors can possess materially different interests.

Recognizing these conflicts does not require treating human beings as enemies whose destruction is desirable.

The purpose of social transformation should be the transformation of unjust relationships and institutions, not the dehumanization of people.

No Political System Is Salvation

Christianity offers Gospel Socialism a final warning against political idolatry.

No market, state, party, movement, class or economic system can perfect humanity. Every institution is operated by imperfect human beings and must therefore remain accountable, limited and open to reform.

The Kingdom of God cannot be reduced to a political programme.

The Gospel determines our ends.
Reason and evidence inform our means.
Democracy restrains our power.
Justice directs our institutions.
Love prevents our struggle for justice from becoming hatred.

For the Common Good

We seek neither the triumph of capital nor the domination of the state.

We seek a society in which economic power is dispersed, work possesses dignity, democracy reaches into economic life, prosperity is broadly shared, communities exercise meaningful agency and institutions exist for the human beings they serve.

Faith gives us reason to seek justice. Truth teaches us how. Love tells us whom it must serve.
